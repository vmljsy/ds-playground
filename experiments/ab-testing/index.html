A/B Testing & Two-Sample Inference

Real-world experimentation in production: conversion lift, Welch's t-tests, and the sequential peeking hazard

How Two-Sample Experimentation Works

In an A/B test, users are randomly assigned to either the Control (A) baseline or the Treatment (B) variant. Depending on the metric, statistical inference tests whether the observed difference is real or merely sampling noise:

$$\text{Proportions (z-Test):} \quad z = \frac{\hat{p}_B - \hat{p}_A}{\sqrt{\hat{p}_{\text{pool}}(1-\hat{p}_{\text{pool}})\left(\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}\right)}} \quad \sim N(0, 1)$$ $$\text{Continuous Means (Welch's t-Test):} \quad t = \frac{\bar{X}_B - \bar{X}_A}{\sqrt{\frac{s_A^2}{n_A} + \frac{s_B^2}{n_B}}} \quad \sim t(df)$$

Difference Confidence Interval

$$\text{Lift CI:} \quad (\hat{\theta}_B - \hat{\theta}_A) \pm z^* \cdot SE_{\text{diff}}$$ If the confidence interval for the difference does not contain zero, the observed lift is statistically significant at significance level $\alpha$.

Practical Rules & Experimentation Pitfalls

The Sequential "Peeking" Problem:
Observing p-values continuously and declaring a winner as soon as $p < 0.05$ creates an optical illusion of significance. Random stochastic walk guarantees that even when there is zero real difference, the p-value will dip below 0.05 at some point during sample collection over 30% of the time!
Sample Size & Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE):
Never stop an experiment early or run it without an a priori power calculation. Determine your required sample size $N$ upfront based on baseline rate, desired statistical power ($80\%$), and the smallest business lift worth detecting.
